Extended Euclidean Algorithm

Calculate the GCD of two integers and determine the Bézout coefficients using the Extended Euclidean Algorithm.

Aim & Objectives

Aim: Implement the Extended Euclidean Algorithm to compute the greatest common divisor (GCD) of two integers and find the Bézout coefficients that satisfy Bézout's identity.

Objectives:

  • Understand the Euclidean Algorithm for computing GCD of two integers.
  • Extend the algorithm to compute Bézout coefficients (x, y) such that a×x + b×y = gcd(a, b).
  • Visualize the step-by-step division process of the Euclidean Algorithm.
  • Verify Bézout's identity for given input values.
  • Understand the relationship between Bézout coefficients and modular multiplicative inverses.
  • Apply the algorithm to cryptographic contexts (RSA key generation, modular inverses).

Theory

Section 1

The Euclidean Algorithm — finding the GCD

The greatest common divisor (GCD) of two integers is the largest positive integer that divides both of them exactly. For example, the divisors shared by 240 and 46 are 1 and 2, so gcd(240, 46) = 2.

The Euclidean Algorithm finds the GCD using only ordinary division. Every division can be written as:

dividend = divisor × quotient + remainder

Walk through the algorithm for 240 and 46. Use Next and Previous (or the ← / → arrow keys) to reveal one division at a time.

Division 1 of 5
  1. 240 = 46 × 5 + 10

    Divide 240 by 46: the quotient is 5 and the remainder is 10.

  2. 46 = 10 × 4 + 6

    The previous divisor 46 is now the dividend, and the previous remainder 10 is now the divisor.

  3. 10 = 6 × 1 + 4

    Divide 10 by 6: quotient 1, remainder 4.

  4. 6 = 4 × 1 + 2

    Divide 6 by 4: quotient 1, remainder 2.

  5. 4 = 2 × 2 + 0

    Divide 4 by 2: the remainder is 0, so the algorithm stops here.

  • Dividend
  • Divisor
  • Quotient
  • Remainder

The final non-zero remainder is 2, so gcd(240, 46) = 2.

Why the divisor and remainder move along

If a number divides both the dividend and the divisor, it must also divide the remainder (because remainder = dividend − divisor × quotient). And if a number divides both the divisor and the remainder, it must also divide the dividend. So the pair (dividend, divisor) and the pair (divisor, remainder) share exactly the same common divisors — which is why the algorithm may replace the first pair by the second without changing the GCD.

Why the last non-zero remainder is the GCD

Each step keeps the GCD the same while making the numbers smaller, so the process must eventually reach a remainder of zero. The step just before that is 6 = 4 × 1 + 2, and the final step is 4 = 2 × 2 + 0. This last step shows that 2 divides 4 exactly, and the step before shows that 2 divides 6. Working upwards, 2 divides every earlier number too, so it is a common divisor. No larger number can work, so 2 is the greatest common divisor.

Section 2

The Extended Euclidean Algorithm — finding x and y

The Euclidean Algorithm gives us the GCD, but it throws away the quotients. The Extended Euclidean Algorithm (EEA) keeps that information and finds two integers x and y such that:

a × x + b × y = gcd(a, b)
a, b
the two integers we start with (here 240 and 46).
x
an integer, possibly negative, that multiplies a.
y
an integer, possibly negative, that multiplies b.
gcd(a, b)
the greatest common divisor of a and b.

The method is called extended because after finishing the ordinary divisions we run them backwards, substituting each remainder into the one above it until the GCD is written purely in terms of a and b. Follow that backward substitution for the same 240 and 46 example.

Substitution 1 of 7
  1. 2 = 6 − 4 × 1

    Start from the second-to-last division 6 = 4 × 1 + 2, then rearrange it to make 2 the subject.

  2. 4 = 10 − 6 × 1

    From 10 = 6 × 1 + 4, express 4 in terms of 10 and 6.

  3. 2 = 6 − (10 − 6 × 1) × 1 = 6 × 2 − 10

    Replace 4 in the first line with its expression from the second line, and tidy up.

  4. 6 = 46 − 10 × 4

    From 46 = 10 × 4 + 6, express 6 in terms of 46 and 10.

  5. 2 = (46 − 10 × 4) × 2 − 10 = 46 × 2 − 10 × 9

    Replace 6 in the third line. We now have 2 written using 46 and 10.

  6. 10 = 240 − 46 × 5

    From 240 = 46 × 5 + 10, express 10 in terms of 240 and 46.

  7. 2 = 46 × 2 − (240 − 46 × 5) × 9 = 46 × 47 − 240 × 9 = 240 × (−9) + 46 × 47

    Substitute 10 into the previous line. The GCD is now a combination of 240 and 46 only.

Reading the coefficients: x = −9 and y = 47, so 240 × (−9) + 46 × 47 = 2.

Result 240 × (−9) + 46 × 47 = 2  →  x = −9, y = 47

The pair (x, y) is not unique. If (x, y) is one solution, then every solution has the form x' = x + k · (b / gcd) and y' = y − k · (a / gcd) for any integer k. The EEA simply returns the solution produced by the backward substitution.

Section 3

Bézout's identity — the relationship behind the coefficients

Bézout's identity is a mathematical statement: for any integers a and b, there exist integers x and y such that a × x + b × y = gcd(a, b).

The algorithm

A procedure — a finite sequence of division and substitution steps. The Extended Euclidean Algorithm is the procedure that computes the GCD together with suitable coefficients.

The identity

A guarantee — the fact that such coefficients always exist. Bézout's identity is the equation those coefficients satisfy. It tells us a solution exists before we compute it.

The identity does the predicting; the algorithm does the finding. We can test the identity directly by substituting the coefficients the EEA produced. Choose a pair below to see the substitution and whether the result matches the GCD.

Section 4

The modular inverse — undoing multiplication

Working “modulo m” means keeping only the remainder after dividing by m. The modular inverse of a modulo m is a number a⁻¹ that behaves like a reciprocal:

a × a⁻¹ ≡ 1 (mod m)

In other words, a × a⁻¹ leaves remainder 1 when divided by m. It is the modular version of “dividing”, and the Extended Euclidean Algorithm is exactly how we compute it.

Example: 17 modulo 5

Try the inverse candidate 3. Then 17 × 3 = 51 and 51 = 5 × 10 + 1, so 51 ≡ 1 (mod 5). That means 3 is the inverse of 17 modulo 5.

(Equivalently, 17 ≡ 2 (mod 5) and 2 × 3 = 6 ≡ 1 (mod 5).)

Non-example: 12 modulo 8

Here gcd(12, 8) = 4 ≠ 1, so no inverse of 12 exists modulo 8. Any candidate x gives a multiple of 4, never a remainder of 1.

Try x = 3 and see what the remainder is.

Rule A modular inverse of a modulo m exists if and only if a and m are coprime, that is, gcd(a, m) = 1.

Why this matters in cryptography. Modular inverses are used to build and operate public-key systems. For instance, in RSA key generation a private exponent d is obtained as the modular inverse of the public exponent e modulo φ(n). The Extended Euclidean Algorithm is one of the standard tools for that step. This experiment focuses on the algorithm and the inverse itself — it does not implement RSA.

Additional notes

Bézout coefficients — extra properties
  • If gcd(a, b) = 1, then x is the modular multiplicative inverse of a modulo b, and y is the modular inverse of b modulo a.
  • The coefficients can be negative; a modular inverse is usually reported as the positive representative modulo the modulus.
  • Bézout's identity guarantees the existence of such coefficients for any integers a and b (including negatives).
Iterative formulation (the version used in the Simulation tab) 
Initialize: (old_r, r) = (a, b)
            (old_x, x) = (1, 0)
            (old_y, y) = (0, 1)

While r != 0:
    quotient = old_r / r
    (old_r, r) = (r, old_r - quotient * r)
    (old_x, x) = (x, old_x - quotient * x)
    (old_y, y) = (y, old_y - quotient * y)

Result: gcd = old_r, x = old_x, y = old_y

This is the same computation as the backward substitution, but carried out forwards. The Simulation tab uses this iterative form.

Applications in cryptography
  • RSA key generation: computing the private exponent d = e⁻¹ mod φ(n).
  • Elliptic curve cryptography: point operations that require modular inverses.
  • Diffie–Hellman: computing shared secrets.
  • Chinese Remainder Theorem: solving systems of congruences.
Time and space complexity
  • Time: O(log min(a, b)). The number of division steps is bounded by roughly five times the number of digits in the smaller number (Lamé's theorem).
  • Space: O(1) for the iterative form; O(log min(a, b)) for a recursive form, because of the call stack.
Important observations and edge cases
  • The algorithm terminates because the remainders form a strictly decreasing sequence of non-negative integers.
  • If a = 0, then gcd(0, b) = |b| with coefficients x = 0, y = sign(b).
  • If a = 0 and b = 0, the GCD is undefined (conventionally 0, but Bézout coefficients do not exist).
  • Negative inputs are handled by taking absolute values for the GCD computation, then adjusting the signs of the coefficients.

Procedure

  1. Enter the first integer a in the input field.
  2. Enter the second integer b in the input field.
  3. Click the Calculate button to start the Extended Euclidean Algorithm.
  4. The algorithm performs repeated division:
    • Divide the current dividend by the divisor.
    • Record the quotient and remainder.
    • Set the divisor as the new dividend and the remainder as the new divisor.
    • Continue until the remainder becomes zero.
  5. The last non-zero remainder is the GCD(a, b).
  6. Simultaneously, the algorithm tracks Bézout coefficients (x, y) using the recurrence relations.
  7. Observe the step-by-step table showing each division step with dividend, divisor, quotient, and remainder.
  8. View the computed Bézout coefficients x and y.
  9. Verify Bézout's identity: a×x + b×y = gcd(a, b) with actual substituted values.
  10. If gcd(a, b) = 1, examine the Modular Inverse section showing a⁻¹ mod b and b⁻¹ mod a.
  11. Click Clear to reset the simulation and enter new values.
