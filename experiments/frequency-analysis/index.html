Frequency Analysis

Learn to analyze ciphertext and break substitution ciphers using letter frequency distributions.

Aim & Objectives

Aim: To understand and apply the principles of frequency analysis to analyze and attempt to decrypt a monoalphabetic substitution cipher.


Objectives:

  • Understand what frequency analysis is and why it is useful in cryptography.
  • Learn how letter frequencies can help identify possible plaintext substitutions.
  • Analyze a given ciphertext to generate a letter-frequency table and graph.
  • Map ciphertext letters to probable plaintext letters by comparing with standard English frequencies.

Theory

Monoalphabetic Substitution Cipher: A cipher where each letter of the plaintext is always replaced by one fixed letter of the ciphertext. For example, all 'A's might become 'Q's. A Caesar shift, Atbash and Affine cipher are special cases; a general substitution uses any rearrangement of the alphabet as the key.

How many keys are there? A general substitution key is a rearrangement of the 26 letters, so there are 26! (about 4 × 1026) keys. Trying them all is impossible, but the statistics of the language leak through the cipher, which is what frequency analysis exploits.


What is Frequency Analysis?

In any given language, certain letters appear more frequently than others. In English, the letter 'E' is the most common, followed by 'T', 'A', 'O', 'I', 'N', 'S', 'H', and 'R'. Because a monoalphabetic substitution cipher does not change the frequency of letters (it merely re-labels them), we can count the occurrences of each letter in the ciphertext and compare those counts to standard English letter frequencies. The technique was first described by the Arab scholar Al-Kindi in the 9th century.

Frequency Percentage:

Frequency Percentage = (character count / total alphabetic characters) × 100

Why Frequency Analysis Works:

By finding the most common letter in the ciphertext, we can guess that it might represent 'E' or 'T'. The next most common might represent the other. By mapping a few common letters, we can start to see partial words, allowing us to guess the rest using contextual and pattern analysis.

Clues beyond single letters:

  • A one-letter word is almost always A or I.
  • Common short words: THE, AND, OF, TO, IS, IT.
  • Common letter pairs (TH, HE, IN, ER, AN) and the triple THE.
  • Word shapes: a pattern such as abcdcefg (a repeated letter) narrows down which dictionary words can fit.
  • Double letters are usually LL, EE, SS, OO or TT.

Limitations:

Frequency analysis works best on long ciphertexts. In short messages, the letter frequencies may not match average statistical distributions. For instance, a short message might not contain a single 'E'. Therefore, contextual analysis (looking at word lengths, repeated letters, common prefixes/suffixes) is critical to refine the mapping. Polyalphabetic ciphers such as Vigenère flatten the frequencies, and transposition ciphers keep the frequencies but scramble the order, so simple frequency analysis does not break them directly.


How the Auto-Solve button works:

Auto-Solve is a teaching aid that automates the same reasoning. It first tries every Caesar, Atbash and Affine key. Otherwise it matches cipher words against a ranked English dictionary by word pattern, and for long texts that match poorly it falls back to a statistical letter-pair search. Short texts can have more than one valid reading, so always check the result yourself.

Procedure

  1. Open the Simulation tab. Either choose Sample 1 or Sample 2 from the drop-down, or type or paste your own ciphertext. Only the letters A to Z are analysed; spaces and punctuation are kept as they are.
  2. Click Analyze Frequency. The experiment shows the bar chart of ciphertext letter frequencies, the English reference table, a frequency table sorted from most to least common, and the Substitution Mapping Workspace.
  3. Compare the most frequent ciphertext letters with the English reference table (E, T, A, O, I, N, ...). Hover over a bar to see its exact percentage.
  4. In the workspace, type your guessed plaintext letter below each ciphertext letter, for example the most frequent ciphertext letter → E.
  5. Click Apply Mapping. Mapped letters appear in lowercase in the Decrypted Text Preview and unmapped letters stay in capitals, so you can read partial words such as th_ and complete them.
  6. Refine the guesses using word clues: one-letter words (A or I), short words (THE, AND, IS), double letters and common endings (-ING, -ED). Apply the mapping again after each change.
  7. If you are stuck, click Hint. Each click shows the next of three hints, and the cycle then repeats.
  8. Click Auto-Solve to see how a program tackles the same problem. It detects Caesar, Atbash or Affine ciphers, otherwise matches words against a dictionary, and fills the mapping boxes and preview. Read the message under the buttons, because short messages can be wrong or have several valid readings.
  9. For Sample 1 or Sample 2, click Check Answer to verify your mapping. It reports incorrect letters, or confirms when every letter in the message is correct. (This button appears only for unedited samples.)
  10. Click Reset to clear the ciphertext, chart, tables and mapping and start again.
  11. Open the Quiz tab, answer all 10 questions and click Submit Quiz. Use Retry Quiz to try again.
  12. Finally, share your experience in the Feedback tab.

Tip: Try the same message in different lengths. A 300-letter paragraph follows English statistics closely, while a 20-letter message often does not. That difference is the central lesson of this experiment.
